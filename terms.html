Handmade means each one is a bit different

Every piece is cut, built and finished by hand out of real cedar. Grain, color and knots vary from board to board, and the same stain sits differently on different wood. The photographs on this site are of pieces I actually built, so yours will look like them — but it will not be an identical copy, and small differences in grain, tone and marking are normal and expected, not defects.

If you want something as close as possible to a particular photo, say so and I'll pick the board with that in mind.

Things listed in the shop

My custom rate is $45 an hour plus materials at cost plus 25%, with half down to get on the bench. If the job changes along the way, I tell you what that does to the price before I carry on.

Because a custom piece is drawn, measured and cut for you specifically, that time and material is spent whether the order goes ahead or not — which is what the deposit is for. I'd rather say that up front than bury it.

Returns

I don't take returns, and I'd rather say so plainly here than at the worst moment.

The reason is the wood. Every board is different before I ever touch it, and once a piece leaves my shop it starts reacting to wherever it ends up living. Humidity, sun, how cold your winters get, whether it sits under a porch roof or out in the open — a swing on a covered porch here in Owingsville and the same swing on a deck somewhere dry will not be the same piece after a month. I can't put that back on the shelf and sell it to the next person as new, and I wouldn't want to.

So there's no change-of-mind return and no restocking window.

If something isn't right

Nothing leaves my shop that isn't right. I cut it, build it, finish it and look it over before it goes in the box. I don't send out work I'm not willing to put my name on, and that is the part I will stand behind: the piece is correct when it leaves here.

What I don't run is a repair, replace or refund policy. Not because I stop caring the moment it's boxed, but because I won't write down a promise about an outcome I can't see coming. Every situation is genuinely different — what happened, when, how the piece was used and kept, and whether it's something I can put right from a bench in Owingsville.

What I will say, and I mean it: tell me. Call or email, send me a photo, and I'll try, to the best of my ability, to remedy the situation. That's an honest effort rather than a guarantee, and I'd rather give you the honest version than one that reads better and then lets you down.

Some things aren't faults at all, and it's fairer to say so here than to argue about it later: normal variation in the wood — grain, color, knots, how a stain sits on a particular board — is covered further up this page. Neither is ordinary wear, nor damage to a piece that's been left out in the weather.

Looking after it

These are real wood, finished by hand. Left outside in Kentucky weather, cedar will gray and a finish will fade — that's the wood doing what wood does, not a fault. Bring it in over the worst of the winter and it'll last a very long time.

If you want to keep it looking close to the day it arrived, here's what actually matters:

  • Wash it once or twice a year. Mild soap, water, a soft rag or a soft brush. That's it. No pressure washer — it drives water into the grain and lifts the soft fibres, and it'll do more damage in ten seconds than a winter will.
  • Keep it up off wet ground. Cedar handles weather well; what it doesn't like is sitting in standing water or against soaked earth. A couple of pavers or a dry spot on the deck does more for it than any finish.
  • Re-coat every year or two if you want to hold the color. What fades a finish is sunlight, so a fresh coat of an exterior oil or sealer with UV protection in it is what keeps the warmth. It is far easier to top up a finish than to strip one back and start again.
  • Or let it go silver. Plenty of folks prefer it. If that's you, just keep it clean and give it a light sanding when it gets rough — a weathered cedar piece is still a sound cedar piece.
  • If you cover it, use a breathable cover. A plastic tarp traps the damp underneath and that's worse than leaving it out.

None of that is fussy work. A wash in the spring and a coat every other year and one of these will outlast the deck it's sitting on.
